    What am I looking at?

    A real molecule, laid out from real numbers. The helix rises 3.38 Å per base pair and turns 34.3° — ten and a half pairs per turn. The two backbones are 127° apart around the axis, not 180°, which is why one groove is wide and the other is narrow. Get that angle wrong and you lose the reason a protein can read a sequence without opening the helix at all.

    The four views

    The molecule
    The duplex at rest, and what heat does to it. Turn the temperature up and it opens at the ends first, then wherever A·T runs — two hydrogen bonds instead of three. Try it on the TATA box preset.
    Replication
    One fork, two daughter helices. Polymerase can only extend a 3′ end, so one new strand runs smoothly into the fork and the other has to be built backwards in pieces. Those pieces are Okazaki fragments.
    Transcription
    A bubble of fourteen base pairs travelling along the molecule, with a transcript being pushed out sideways so the DNA can shut behind it.
    Translation
    The ribosome, three nucleotides at a time. Wrong tRNAs arrive constantly and fall out; the right one stays. Then the chain folds.

    Where it is honest, and where it is not

    Honest: helical parameters, groove asymmetry, purine and pyrimidine ring counts, two-versus-three hydrogen bonds, antiparallel strands, 5′→3′ synthesis and everything that follows from it, the standard genetic code, 3.8 Å between alpha carbons, and selection by rejection — wrong subunits really do arrive and leave.

    Not: the proteins are lumpy blobs, not structures. Timescales are slowed by a factor of roughly a million. Okazaki fragments are 9 bases here and 1,000–2,000 in a bacterium. The folding is a hydrophobic-collapse toy: it genuinely compacts the chain, from a rod of about 22 Å radius of gyration to a globule of about 11 Å, and with enough chain it does bury the greasy residues — but the peptides here are too short to have an inside, so expect a ball rather than a core. It is not a structure prediction.
